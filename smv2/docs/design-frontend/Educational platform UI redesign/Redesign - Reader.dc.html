Solving Linear Equations - General Equations

Objective: Solve general linear equations with variables on both sides.

Often as we are solving linear equations we will need to do some work to set them up into a form we are familiar with solving. This section will focus on manipulating an equation we are asked to solve in such a way that we can use our pattern for solving two-step equations to ultimately arrive at the solution.

One such issue that needs to be addressed is parenthesis. Often the parenthesis can get in the way of solving an otherwise easy problem. As you might expect we can get rid of the unwanted parenthesis by using the distributive property. This is shown in the following example. Notice the first step is distributing, then it is solved like any other two-step equation.

Example 59.

4(2x − 6) = 16Distribute 4 through parenthesis
8x − 24 = 16Focus on the subtraction first
+ 24 + 24Add 24 to both sides
8x = 40Now focus on the multiply by 8
8    8Divide both sides by 8
x = 5Our Solution!

Often after we distribute there will be some like terms on one side of the equation. Example 2 shows distributing to clear the parenthesis and then combining like terms next.

General equations, in plain language

The big idea: every "scary" equation in this section is just a two-step equation wearing a disguise. Your job is to remove the disguise first — clear parentheses, combine like terms, gather the variable on one side — and then solve it exactly like you did in 1.2.

Undressing order: distribute → combine like terms → move variables to one side → then the familiar two steps: undo add/subtract, undo multiply/divide.

Worked example, narrated: in 4(2x − 6) = 16, the 4 is "holding" the parentheses shut. Distribute it (8x − 24 = 16) and the disguise is off — this is now the same kind of problem as 1.2. Add 24 to both sides, divide by 8, and x = 5.

Where students slip: forgetting to distribute to the second term inside the parentheses, and losing the sign on negative numbers. The sign always stays attached to the number in front of it.

Two-step equations — let's break this down

Where this sits: Section 1.2, right after One-Step Equations — it builds directly on the balancing skill you just learned.

Analogy: think of undoing a coat and then a sweater. Whatever was put on last comes off first — solving reverses the order of operations: add or subtract first, then multiply or divide.

Two rules: whatever you do to one side, do to the other; and the sign always stays attached to the number in front of it.
